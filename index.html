ZSHIRTS

This shirt carries a secret message.

It's written on the Zcash blockchain, encrypted. This page holds most of the key that can read it. The last 16 characters are printed on the shirt.


    

      The characters on the shirt are the message
      
        
        

          
Two things are printed on the shirt

          
The lines of characters are the message, locked. Every pair, like BD, is one letter.

          
The four boxes hold the last 16 characters of the viewing key. A viewing key is one long code, nearly 200 characters. This page has all of it except the end, and the shirt has the end. Neither half opens anything alone.

          
How one letter gets locked

          
    
            
  1. A letter is a number. A computer stores Z as 90, written in hex (digits 0–9 and A–F) as 5A.
    2. 
            
  2. The sender makes secret noise. Their wallet uses ChaCha20 to make a stream of random-looking numbers that only this viewing key can make again. Say the next one is 3C.
    3. 
            
  3. Mix the two. Line up their bits and write 1 wherever they differ (this is called XOR):
              Z      5A = 0101 1010
noise  3C = 0011 1100
locked 66 = 0110 0110
    4. 
            
  4. 66 goes on the blockchain, and on the shirt. Every letter gets a fresh noise number, so two Z's never look alike.
    5. 
          

          
How it unlocks

          
    
            
  1. Your 16 characters complete the viewing key.
    2. 
            
  2. The key makes the exact same noise again: 3C.
    3. 
            
  3. Mix again and the noise cancels out:
              locked 66 = 0110 0110
noise  3C = 0011 1100
Z      5A = 0101 1010
    4. 
          

          
That's what you see when you press Decrypt: each pair flips back into its letter.

          
This is a made-up example, not a letter from the real message. How do the sender and the key end up with the same noise without ever talking? Through a trick called Diffie–Hellman: the transaction carries a public hint that only the viewing key can turn into the secret. A 16-byte seal also proves nobody changed a byte.

What's a viewing key?

            
A read-only key to a Zcash address. It can see what that address received (like this message) but it can't spend a single coin.

            
Sharing it is like letting someone read your mail, not open your wallet.

          

        
      

      

      

        
        
          
          

            
Why 16 characters?

            
Each character is one of 32, so it carries 5 bits. Sixteen of them make 80 bits.

            
The key ends in a checksum that can confirm your typing, but that also means it gives away 30 of those bits to a guesser:

            25×16 − 30 = 250 ≈ 1015 keys
            
That's too many to try by hand, so you need the shirt. The same checksum is why one typo gets fixed: the page tries every single-character swap and keeps the only one that fits.

How it works

    
    
      
  1. This page holds most of a Zcash viewing key. The shirt holds the last 16 characters. Together they make the whole key.
    2. 
      
  2. A viewing key can read what a wallet received. It can never spend. The wearer chose to share it with you.
    3. 
      
  3. Your 16 characters complete the key, and its checksum confirms them (one typo gets fixed). The key finds the note sent to the shirt's address and unscrambles the bytes printed on the shirt.
    4. 
      
  4. The key is scrambled before it's written out, so those 16 characters aren't one particular part of it. The page needs every character of it.
    5.
